How I made TICK TOCK: the method and a starter prompt

A 36-second wordless story reel: a thief and a real-looking tiny fairy steal a hook-handed boss's gold watches, and a crocodile that eats watches ends up on their side. Here are the 7 steps and a prompt to start your own.

The format comes from the fairy-tale-in-real-life reels in this niche: no dialogue, one rap track, a quiet start, then the drop. I rebuilt it with my own thief, my own tiny fairy and a crocodile that eats watches.

What you need

  • An image tool with Nano Banana Pro. I used Higgsfield. You paste a prompt, add a reference photo and generate.
  • An image-to-video tool with sound. I used Seedance 2.5 Draft (480p) through Higgsfield. You upload a still and it moves, with sound.
  • Any editor. Hard cuts, a few slow-motion beats and a song. CapCut is fine.

7 steps

  1. Story first, with one rule people can see

    Pick the last picture before anything else. Mine is a crocodile grinning with a tiny gold watch on every tooth. Then give the world one rule that a viewer understands with the sound off. Mine: the crocodile eats anything that ticks. Show the rule early, so the ending pays off.

  2. One master photo per character

    Make a few photoreal portraits of each character and pick one. This is the face and body every other frame copies. Write it like a real film still: real skin, pores, freckles, no "CGI" words. For a real-looking tiny fairy, ask for "the real proportions of an adult woman, small head, real skin and hair, a real miniature person, not a doll".

  3. Tie the sidekick's size to a real object

    A tiny character in a wide shot turns into a few pixels and looks like a cartoon sprite. Put her close, in contact with something real, and say how big she is against it: "as tall as the crocodile's eye is wide", "the size of a matchbox". Put her in every shot, or make sure we hear her.

  4. Start frames: one edit per shot, from the master

    Every shot begins as a still. Make each still in one edit from the master photo or the location photo: change only the place, the pose and the framing. Do not chain three edits on top of each other, because faces turn plastic. Look at each still before you animate it: count the people, check the props.

  5. One shot per generation, with sound on

    Generate 4 seconds per clip, at 480p draft, with native sound on. One action and one camera move per clip. Write the sounds you want and ban music: "No music. Only realistic sound tied to what is visible: ..." The clips cost the same with sound on, and the little noises sell the shot.

  6. Edit on the beat of the song

    Choose a track with a quiet start and a drop. Put your cuts on its beats, about 0.65 seconds apart. Keep the calm moments (the crocodile being tamed, the tooth polishing) in slow motion and use hard cuts and speed-ups for the chase. Turn the clips' own sound very low under the music, and use a black full-screen title card for the time jump.

  7. Finish: upscale, grade, no clutter

    Upscale the 480p clips, darken and warm the colour a little, and add no grain. Skip captions on a wordless story. Put the call to action on the last shot only.

Plan 12 shots. One shot per generation, 4 seconds each. For every shot give me:
1) the start-frame description (the sidekick is in every shot or is heard);
2) ONE action in plain words and ONE camera move;
3) the sounds I should hear, with no music.
Make the creature's rule visible with the sound off, and end on a payoff that reads without sound.
Show me the plan and the credit estimate before generating anything.

Swap the four brackets: hero, size of the sidekick, creature, story. Ask for the shot list first and generate nothing until you like it.

Common mistakes

  • Three shots in one long clip. My first try was 9 seconds with three shots and no sound. Each shot got under 3 seconds, so the run looked like a fast walk and nothing made sense. One shot per generation fixed it.
  • A tiny character in a wide shot. At 480p the fairy became a few pixels, with no wing beats and no face. Keep her close to a real object, tie her size to it, and keep her in every shot.
  • Editing a frame to shrink someone already in it. The image tool kept the same size in two tries. Start from the frame without her, add her again and say how big she is next to a real object.
