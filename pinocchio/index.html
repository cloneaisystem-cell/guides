How I made Pinocchio in real life: the prompts and the edit

A 27-second reel where Pinocchio does modern money crimes in Italy, played by a real-looking young actor with a long wooden nose. Here are the 6 steps, the prompts I pasted and the exact cut times.

The format comes from @tapewarp.ai's reels with fairy-tale characters in real life: no dialogue, one phonk track, a quiet start, then the drop. I rebuilt it with my own character and story.

What you need

  • An image tool with Nano Banana Pro. I used Higgsfield. You paste a prompt, add a reference image and generate.
  • An image-to-video tool. I used Seedance 2.5 Draft on Dreamina, in first-frame mode (you upload a still and it moves).
  • Any editor. CapCut is fine. You only need hard cuts, a zoom and a song.

6 steps

  1. Character and story: ending first

    Start from the last picture. Mine is a police mugshot, and it is also the first thing you see in the reel. Then give the character 6 to 8 modern crimes that are puns on who he is. Each pun becomes one shot.
    • Money launderinga washing machine on a lawn, full of banknotes
    • Drying the cashwet banknotes pegged along his long nose like a clothesline
    • The nose gives him awayit pierces the newspaper of the old carpenter, Geppetto
    • Shopping spreehandbags hung on the nose in a boutique
    • The bankan ID card pushed across the teller's counter
    • The endingarrested by police in a palace garden, champagne in both hands

    One rule saves the whole video: the nose never grows on camera. It is short in one shot and long in the next, and the change happens on the cut. A nose that grows inside a clip looks fake.

  2. The master actor portrait

    Make one picture of a real-looking actor in costume, not a CGI puppet. This is the face every other shot copies. In Nano Banana Pro, 4:3, 2K, generate a few and pick one. My template, with the parts you swap in brackets: 
    Photoreal live-action film still from a 2000s European fairy-tale movie, 4:3. A real young actor, about 19 years old, slim, boyish face with real skin, freckles and visible pores, plays [CHARACTER] in costume: [FILM MAKEUP, e.g. subtle makeup that gives his face a faint painted-wood sheen with light wood-grain lines and two small round rosy painted spots on his cheeks]; [SIGNATURE FEATURE, e.g. a long pointed wooden prosthetic nose, about 15 cm, pale raw pine, attached seamlessly]. He wears [COSTUME, e.g. a crumpled folded-paper cap with a crease line, a loose suit of faded flower-printed paper under an open black puffer jacket, a thin gold chain, and shoes made of tree bark]. He stands in [PLACE, e.g. a small sunny Tuscan piazza; real people walk past and ignore him]. 35mm prime, eye level, medium shot, late-morning sun from frame left. Real film look, slightly soft, true blacks, bright whites, natural muted colours, no grain, no text.
    My exact Pinocchio prompt

    Why an actor: the reels I copied use real people in costume. Make him about 19 rather than a child, and write the prop (here the nose) as film makeup, so it reads as a real object.

  3. The character sheet

    Open your chosen master, press Reference, paste the common block, then add one framing line and generate. One image per run. You get the same actor from every side, and you will use these as references later. 
    Use @Image1 as the exact same actor and costume: same face, freckles, skin, hair, [MAKEUP DETAILS, e.g. wood-grain makeup lines, rosy painted spot on the cheek, marionette jaw lines], [SIGNATURE FEATURE, e.g. the same long pale-pine prosthetic nose], the same [COSTUME, e.g. folded newspaper cap, flower-printed paper suit, black puffer jacket and thin gold chain]. Plain light-grey studio backdrop, soft even daylight from frame left. Real film look, natural colours, no grain, no text.

    Then one of these, added after the block:

    FRONT (4:3): New framing only: head-and-shoulders close-up, facing the camera straight on, neutral calm expression, eyes to the lens.
THREE-QUARTER (4:3): New framing only: head-and-shoulders, head turned three-quarters to frame-right so the nose shows in silhouette against the grey backdrop, eyes looking past the camera.
PROFILE (4:3): New framing only: exact side profile facing frame-right, head and shoulders, the full length of the wooden nose clearly visible against the plain backdrop.
FULL BODY (3:4): New framing only: full body standing, front view, arms relaxed, the whole costume visible down to the tree-bark shoes.
LONG NOSE (4:3): Same as the three-quarter framing, but change only the nose: it is now about 60 cm long, as long as his whole arm, a perfectly straight rigid rod of pale raw pine, pointing toward frame-right. Same face and expression.

    Show the long nose from the side or at three-quarters. Seen from the front, it gets short and strange.

  4. Start frames: one edit per shot

    Every shot starts as a still, and each still is an edit of the master. You change only the place and the pose. The face, body and costume stay as they are. Here is the washing machine shot: 
    Use @Image1 as the exact same actor and costume: same face, freckles, skin, hair, wood-grain makeup lines, rosy painted spot on the cheek, marionette jaw lines, the same short wooden nose, the same folded newspaper cap, flower-printed paper suit, black puffer jacket and thin gold chain.
New scene and pose only: he kneels on the grass of a sunny Tuscan garden behind an old stone farmhouse, both hands resting on his knees, eyes on the round glass door of an old white front-loading washing machine that stands on the lawn, an orange extension cord running across the grass. The drum behind the glass is full of euro banknotes. Wide shot, eye level, 35mm, warm low sun from frame left. Real film look, natural colours, no grain, no text.

    Make one 4:3 still per shot. Look at each one before you animate it: count the people, check the props and check the nose.

  5. Video: short, cheap clips

    In Seedance 2.5 Draft, 480p, 4:3, first-frame mode: upload the still and paste the prompt. Make each clip 4 to 5 seconds. You will only use 1 to 2 seconds of it in the edit, so Draft quality is enough. I paid about 52 credits per 4 s clip on Dreamina's plan. I made 15 clips and 14 made the cut. The one that failed was a straight-on face: the long nose turned toward the lens.

    Each prompt is three blocks pasted in one go: GLOBAL (the same every time), SHOT (what moves in this clip) and END (the same every time).

    GLOBAL, pasted first:

    STYLE & CAMERA: photoreal live-action 4:3 film, a real 2000s Italian comedy movie shot on 35mm, soft DVD-era image, real actors in costume. Lighting exactly as in the first frame. Real-time speed, no slow motion, no speed ramp. Crash zoom = the lens zooms in very fast, camera position fixed, heavy motion blur on the move only, settles sharp in about 0.4 s. Whip pan = the camera pans fast with horizontal motion blur, then settles. Between moves the picture is sharp and stable.
START-FRAME LOCK: the uploaded image is the exact first frame: same framing, light, wardrobe, props and positions. Already moving on frame 1 (see the beats). Only people and objects visible in it exist; nothing new is added except what a beat names.

    SHOT, example 1, the washing machine (4 s):

    SUMMARY: crash zoom from a kneeling young man into the porthole of a washing machine full of banknotes on a lawn.
SHOT 1, wide shot, handheld 35mm with a small sway. [0-1.2s] The YOUNG MAN, kneeling on the grass with his hands on his knees, rocks forward half an inch, eyes on the round glass door; grass and the orange cord stay as in frame 1. [1.2-2.2s] Crash zoom (lens zoom, camera position fixed) at the porthole with heavy motion blur until the glass fills the frame. [2.2-4s] Close-up through the glass: the drum turns slowly and wet euro banknotes tumble inside, a few water drops run down the glass, sharp focus. His short wooden nose keeps its frame-1 length.

    SHOT, example 2, the woman in the limousine (4 s):

    SUMMARY: the woman with turquoise hair slides her sunglasses down inside the limousine, then the camera whips away.
SHOT 1, close-up through the open window, handheld 35mm, shallow depth of field. [0-2s] The TURQUOISE WOMAN, her black-gloved finger already at her sunglasses, slides them down her nose, her gaze fixed on the street at frame-left. [2-3.3s] Her gaze turns to the lens and one corner of her mouth lifts; the head stays almost still. [3.3-4s] The camera whip-pans right with heavy motion blur. Her hair, fur coat and the car window frame stay as in frame 1.

    END, pasted last:

    PHYSICS: five fingers per hand; the wooden nose is one rigid prosthetic piece glued to the face and keeps the exact length and shape it has in frame 1 for the whole clip.
AUDIO: room ambience only. Nobody speaks, all mouths stay closed. No music, no singing.
EXCLUDE: subtitles, captions, on-screen text, brand logos, extra people, morphing, CGI look, slow motion, a second nose, a bent nose.

    The rules I learned the hard way:

    1. Don't describe what the picture already shows. Write only what moves. If the frame has sunglasses on, "puts on sunglasses" confuses the model.
    2. Whip pans and crash zooms go in the middle or at the end of the clip, never at 0 s. The first frame is a sharp still.
    3. Frame 0 should already be moving: bubbles, a fountain, leaves, an eye. A "nobody moves" start freezes the clip.
    4. Write the nose length once, in positive words: "keeps exactly its frame-1 length and shape". Never write "doesn't bend". Never let a hand or a strap touch it.
    5. Count the people: "Exactly four people". Otherwise the model adds a fifth.
    6. Use body cues, not feelings. "Eyebrows raised, lips pressed" works. "Pleased" or "proudly" doesn't.
    7. Define "crash zoom" in GLOBAL. Without it the model turns it into a slow push-in.

  6. The edit

    Put the 12 clips on a timeline over the song, 14 shots in total, with hard cuts only. The song's drop lands on the first crime, at about 5 seconds. Before that, the quiet intro runs under the mugshot and the car. Cut at these times, in seconds:

    • Show the ending first. The mugshot is the first shot, so people know where it goes.
    • Keep the soft 480p look. I upscaled it a little and left it soft. That is the film look.
    • Put your handle on the last frame.
    • The song: pick a phonk track with a quiet intro and a drop around 5 seconds. I added mine in the Instagram music library, so the video file has no sound.

Common mistakes

  • The nose bends or droops. You wrote the nose with a negative ("doesn't bend"), or something touched it. State its length once in positive words, keep it horizontal, and keep hands, straps and bags off it.
  • A face looking straight at the camera with a horizontal nose. The nose turns toward the lens and the face morphs. In my tests the nose turned toward the lens on a straight-on face. Show the long nose from the side or at three-quarters.
  • Cuts that jump back in time look broken. If shot B shows the money before shot A hands it over, viewers feel it without knowing why. Cut in story order.
  • The model invents a new angle. A hard cut inside a clip makes it guess the second angle and sometimes change the actor. Use one shot per clip, and cut in your editor.
  • The first frame is blurry or settles. A whip pan at 0 s can't work. Move the camera to the second half.
